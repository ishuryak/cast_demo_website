01 / The choice

The claim picks the tool.

Suppose you want to say “treatment cut the event rate by 30 percent.” That is one number for the whole follow-up, and Cox gives it cheaply, provided the hazards stay in a constant ratio and one effect fits everyone. Suppose instead you want to say “16 more people in 100 were alive at three years, and the gain was gone by nine.” That is a curve, and CAST gives it, provided you have measured the confounders and will fit models for treatment, censoring and survival. The data can be the same. The claim and the assumptions are not.

On the surveyor’s bench, two trails on a map stand for survival under treatment and under control. The dividers hold one fixed gap: on the log(−log S) scale, proportional hazards is exactly that constant gap (the check in section 02). The French curve follows whatever shape the gap takes: CAST’s trajectory of survival differences over time.

Cox proportional hazards

Assumes
One decision fixed at baseline. Hazards in a constant ratio over follow-up. One effect that applies to everyone.
Returns
One hazard ratio: a relative summary, read as “the treated group’s event rate is 0.7 times the control group’s, throughout follow-up.”
Needs
Modest data. Measured confounders in the model.

Causal survival forests (CSF), then CAST

Assumes
One decision fixed at baseline. Measured confounders, overlap, and censoring explained by baseline covariates. CSF assumes nothing about the shape of the effect over time or across people; CAST assumes the trajectory across horizons is close to a quadratic.
Returns
Survival differences at chosen horizons, in percentage points, smoothed into a curve: “+16 points alive at 36 months, +9 at 60, −6 at 108.”
Needs
More events. A propensity model, a censoring model and a survival model, and an honest look at the curve’s uncertainty.

Both need the comparison to be causal in the first place (section 07).

02 / When one number is enough

Cox’s home ground.

When the treatment is a single decision at baseline, the hazards stay in a constant ratio, and one effect describes everyone, a single hazard ratio is the right summary: efficient, modest in its data needs, and familiar. Three checks tell you whether those conditions hold.

  1. 1
    Fixed at baseline. Treatment is decided once, at the start. If the question is about a strategy that changes with the patient’s course, rather than the decision made at baseline, no baseline model answers it; see the longitudinal branch in section 06.
  2. 2
    Proportional hazards. The log(−log S) curves stay parallel and the Schoenfeld residuals show no trend with time. Crossing curves or late separation fail this check.
  3. 3
    One effect for everyone. Profiles do not respond in different directions or on different timescales. Where they do, one ratio averages over the difference you wanted to see.
What a hazard ratio is, and is not Relative, non-collapsible, and silent about time

A hazard ratio compares event rates among people still at risk. Even in a randomized trial, the people still at risk after baseline differ between arms, because treatment changed who survived to that point; a hazard ratio after time zero therefore mixes the effect with that selection. It is relative: it does not say how many more people are alive at a given time. It is non-collapsible: adjusting for a prognostic covariate changes it even when that covariate is not a confounder. And when the effect changes over time, the single number averages over the follow-up in a way that depends on the censoring pattern, so two studies of the same treatment can report different ratios without either being wrong.

Survival differences at named horizons, the scale CSF and CAST report, are absolute and have a direct decision reading. Section 06 returns to this.

03 / When the story changes over time

The average changes.

Under “early benefit, later reversal” the survival difference starts positive and ends negative. A single hazard ratio averages a benefit and a harm into one number, and which number depends on how long people were followed. The horizon estimates keep the two apart. The fitted trajectory estimates where one turns into the other, and smooths: in this cohort it places the crossing about twenty months later than the truth. Reveal the truth to see this.

The simulation carries a Cox model too. “Benefit throughout follow-up” is Cox’s home ground: that scenario is generated from a constant hazard ratio, and the Cox fit lands on the truth (hazard ratio 0.54, proportional-hazards test p = 0.22). Under the reversal the same model returns 0.87 with a failed proportionality test, and the survival differences it implies stay between +1 and +4 points while the truth runs from +16 to −6. 04 / From slices to a curve

Slices first, then the curve.

Each dot in the chart above is a CSF estimate of the survival benefit at one horizon. CAST (Causal Analysis for Survival Trajectories) fits a curve to these estimates to summarize how that benefit changes during follow-up.

The quadratic curve is fitted by generalized least squares, weighting the five estimates by their estimated covariance across horizons. Values between horizons depend on this fitted model.

From slices to a curve The intuition in a sketch

The drawing’s “OR then pool” note sketches a different modeling route. This demo estimates survival-probability differences at each horizon, then fits their trajectory. An odds ratio is a different contrast. The drawing is an intuition for time slices, not an algorithm for CAST or LTMLE.

Check the intervals and the smooth band separately.

05 / When people differ

How does benefit vary across people?

One hazard ratio is one number for everyone, and the cohort average can obscure differences between groups. A dose that helps one group may help another less, and those dose–response curves can change over time.

In this simulation the hazard ratio is the same for everyone, so survival benefit differs across profiles only through baseline risk. In real data a causal survival forest estimates profile-specific differences directly, where a Cox model needs interaction terms chosen in advance.

τ(a, t, x) = E[Yᵃ(t) − Y⁰(t) | X = x]

Here a is a specified baseline dose, t is follow-up time, and x describes baseline characteristics. This motivating target compares dose a with zero dose on the same outcome scale. A sequence of doses chosen as health changes defines a different intervention, the longitudinal branch in section 06.

06 / Which tool, when?

Three questions.

For a clinician or a patient, the calendar is usually the answer wanted; the ratio is the one most papers report. CSF and CAST work on the calendar scale by construction. A Cox model can be turned into survival curves and differences too, provided its assumptions hold.

The longitudinal branch.

Longitudinal analysis follows people through time. It can describe changing outcomes; causal longitudinal analysis asks how those outcomes would change under different treatment choices or histories.

We might compare one decision at the start and follow its effects over time, or study decisions that change during follow-up in response to a person’s history. Everything above is the first kind; the second needs different tools.

Two shapes of question

Follow a baseline treatment decision.

Compare survival under treatment and control at several times after the baseline decision.

This demo asks how survival benefit changes after a baseline treatment decision.

Adapt treatment as new information arrives.

A treatment rule can respond to changing health measurements at each visit. Earlier treatment can affect those measurements and subsequent decisions.

Here the target is an outcome under a treatment history or policy. LTMLE is one approach to estimating such targets.

At decision 2, read the characteristic as L₂. This threshold sketch illustrates a dynamic rule; a modified treatment policy can additionally depend on the treatment value that would naturally occur.

Related approaches Where TMLE, LTMLE, LMTP and CAST meet
TMLEAn estimation framework
Targeted maximum likelihood (or minimum loss-based) estimation updates an initial estimate toward a specified target. It can combine flexible prediction with causal estimation under appropriate assumptions.
LTMLELongitudinal estimation
Applies targeted estimation through an ordered history of measurements, treatments and censoring. It can estimate outcomes under specified treatment regimes, including rules that respond to evolving covariates.
LMTPA class of intervention policies
Longitudinal modified treatment policies can modify a naturally occurring treatment value using the available history, for example a specified dose reduction where supported. Their effects can be estimated using TMLE or sequentially doubly robust estimators.
CSF → CASTThe survival branch explored here
Causal survival forests supply effect estimates at chosen horizons. CAST models their trajectory. This demonstration follows a binary baseline decision and displays cohort averages.

TMLE-based methods can also address baseline treatments and survival outcomes. For repeated decisions, treatment–confounder feedback may also need to be addressed. The choice of estimator depends on the target and assumptions about treatment assignment, support and censoring.

07 / Why any of this is causal

Why “adjusted” is not yet “causal”.

Every tool above compares survival under treatment with survival under control. Neither Cox nor CAST makes that comparison causal on its own. That takes three things: one person has two possible paths and we only ever see one; the treated and untreated groups differed before treatment; and whether the data can answer the question is settled before any estimator is chosen.

07 · The missing path

Pause both paths at the same time.

Hector’s treatment effect would compare his two possible outcomes at the same time. Only the path he takes can be observed. His improvement since treatment began cannot tell us what would have happened without it.

Across people, we can ask about an average effect, even though each person’s alternative remains unobserved. Hector’s sketch shows symptoms. The worked example in section 03 uses survival: how does treatment change the probability of being alive at a chosen time?

A difference of +10 percentage points means survival is 10 points more likely under treatment at that time. It does not tell us which people benefited.

A different kind of missing How this connects to MCAR, MAR and MNAR

A missing lab result is a value we might have recorded along the observed path. Hector’s outcome under the other treatment belongs to a path he did not take. Even a perfectly complete record leaves that alternative unobserved.

Causal inference can itself be formulated as a missing-data problem: treatment assignment determines which potential outcome we observe. Assumptions can let us identify an average contrast without recovering everyone’s missing outcome.

MCAR
Missingness does not depend on the data values.
MAR
Given observed values, missingness does not further depend on the missing values.
MNAR
That MAR condition fails; missingness still depends on unobserved values.

Read the sketch’s collider example separately: selection bias and MNAR are not synonyms. MAR can be informative about observed variables; it does not mean “nothing predicts missingness.”

Thought experiment One average, different individual effects

These ten invented records illustrate the missing counterfactual at one fixed horizon, with no censoring. This thought experiment uses its own teaching data. Start with the observed records, then compare two possible completions of what is missing.

Bold teal outcomes are observed. Question marks are missing. Dotted, italic outcomes are hypothetical completions.

We observe 3 of 5 treated people alive and 2 of 5 control people alive. That observed contrast alone does not identify the causal effect. Ten alternative outcomes remain missing.

2 helped · 0 harmed · 8 unchanged.
If all ten received treatment, 6 would survive; under control, 4 would survive. The sample average effect is (6 − 4) / 10 = +20 percentage points.

4 helped · 2 harmed · 4 unchanged.
Again, 6 would survive under treatment and 4 under control: +20 percentage points. The same average hides a different set of individual effects.

07 · A fair comparison

Who receives treatment matters.

In the simulation in section 03 (tick “Compare without adjustment”), healthier people are more likely to receive treatment when confounding is present. They also tend to survive longer. A direct comparison can mistake some of that advantage for a treatment effect.

Like a Cox model, a causal survival forest adjusts for measured baseline characteristics and accounts for censoring, when follow-up ends before the event is observed. Neither makes the comparison causal on its own; that depends on the study design and assumptions.

07 · From a question to data

Can these data answer it?

Before choosing an estimator, specify the question, the available data and the assumptions connecting them. Identification asks whether the causal quantity can be expressed in terms of the observed data distribution.

A causal diagram can express part of that contract. We also need well-defined treatments, relevant treatment support, assumptions about confounding and censoring, and enough information for useful precision.

The causal roadmap organizes these steps, from defining the question through identification, estimation and interpretation. Cox, CSF and CAST all belong to the estimation step.
