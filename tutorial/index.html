Start here

Did the treatment help, or were those patients healthier to begin with?

A walkthrough on simulated cancer cohorts where the true answer is known, so you can watch each method succeed or fail against it. Built for residents and clinicians first. Useful to students, researchers and educators.

Is this for you?

You read survival comparisons from registries, records or papers and want to know how far to trust them. You know what a survival curve is and roughly what a confounder is. Everything else is introduced on the way, in about the time it takes to read a long article.

If you came for the estimators and the full grading, go straight to the methods page.

What you will be able to do

  • Read an observed survival gap with the right suspicion: how much is the treatment, how much is who received it.
  • Read a survival-difference curve at the follow-up time that matters for a patient, in survivors per 100, and say what the band does and does not promise.
  • Ask the right questions of a paper, a registry or a biostatistician before trusting an adjusted comparison.

Three ways through

  • Ten minutes. Chapters 1 and 2: the story and the puzzle.
  • A 45-minute class. Chapters 1 to 5, with the class kit for the hands-on part.
  • The whole thing. Every chapter, then the methods page and the repository.

What this is not

Every patient here is simulated and the two treatment options are abstract. No method is crowned, and nothing is validated on patient data. That is the point: with a known answer key, each method can be graded against the truth instead of against the other methods, which is the one comparison no real study can run.

Chapter 1

Hector and Bob.

Hector and Bob have the same cancer at the same stage, and both are candidates for either of two treatment plans. Hector is younger, walks into clinic without help and has one other condition on his chart. Bob is older, tires easily and has three. Hector’s team chooses the more intensive plan; call it Option A. Bob’s team chooses Option B.

Three years later Hector is alive. Bob died twenty months after treatment began.

Put two thousand Hectors and Bobs into a registry and the chart draws itself: at three years, about 32 more survivors per 100 among those who received A. It looks like A works. The rest of this walkthrough takes that chart apart, one layer at a time.

The first layer is visible once it is named: who receives A. Fitter patients, who would have lived longer on either plan. The second layer is invisible: what would have happened to Bob on A, or to Hector on B. The third is time. In this simulated population the true advantage of A is about 16 survivors per 100 at three years and about 2 fewer at seven; a single hazard ratio cannot say both things. The fourth is what no method can fix: a fitness nobody wrote down.

Hector and Bob are invented, and so is every patient here. Their characteristics are the ones the simulation uses: age, stage, performance status and comorbidity count. That is deliberate. The story carries the same confounders the methods have to handle. The numbers come from the chapter 2 cohort.

Pause both paths at the same time.

Hector’s treatment effect would compare his two possible outcomes at the same time. Only the path he takes can be observed. His improvement since treatment began cannot tell us what would have happened without it.

Across people, we can ask about an average effect, even though each person’s alternative remains unobserved. Hector’s sketch shows symptoms. The worked example turns to survival: how does treatment change the probability of being alive at a chosen time?

A difference of +10 percentage points means survival is 10 points more likely under treatment at that time. It does not tell us which people benefited.

A different kind of missing How this connects to MCAR, MAR and MNAR

A missing lab result is a value we might have recorded along the observed path. Hector’s outcome under the other treatment belongs to a path he did not take. Even a perfectly complete record leaves that alternative unobserved.

Causal inference can itself be formulated as a missing-data problem: treatment assignment determines which potential outcome we observe. Assumptions can let us identify an average contrast without recovering everyone’s missing outcome.

MCAR
Missingness does not depend on the data values.
MAR
Given observed values, missingness does not further depend on the missing values.
MNAR
That MAR condition fails; missingness still depends on unobserved values.

Read the sketch’s collider example separately: selection bias and MNAR are not synonyms. MAR can be informative about observed variables; it does not mean “nothing predicts missingness.”

Thought experiment One average, different individual effects

These ten invented records illustrate the missing counterfactual at one fixed horizon, with no censoring. This thought experiment uses its own teaching data. Start with the observed records, then compare two possible completions of what is missing.

Bold teal outcomes are observed. Question marks are missing. Dotted, italic outcomes are hypothetical completions.

We observe 3 of 5 treated people alive and 2 of 5 control people alive. That observed contrast alone does not identify the causal effect. Ten alternative outcomes remain missing.

2 helped · 0 harmed · 8 unchanged.
If all ten received treatment, 6 would survive; under control, 4 would survive. The sample average effect is (6 − 4) / 10 = +20 percentage points.

4 helped · 2 harmed · 4 unchanged.
Again, 6 would survive under treatment and 4 under control: +20 percentage points. The same average hides a different set of individual effects.

Chapter 3

Read it like a clinician.

A method question is a clinical question in disguise. Each reading below turns one into the other: what to ask at tumor board, what to look for in a paper, and what to say to the biostatistician before an adjusted registry comparison is trusted. The chart in chapter 4 lets you try each one.

Two active options

The comparison can be between two active treatment plans. In the survival example, read “treatment” as Option A and “control” as Option B; either may be an active option.

Read a little further

The opening Hector sketch illustrates treatment versus no treatment. The same counterfactual question applies to two active options: what would happen in the same target population under each plan? Define eligibility, both plans and the start of follow-up before making the comparison.

A difference of +10 percentage points at five years means about 10 additional survivors per 100 in expectation under A versus B at that horizon. It does not identify which people benefit or imply a permanent survival gain.

Who receives which option?

In this simulation, fitter patients are more likely to receive Option A and tend to live longer either way. The observed survival gap mixes treatment effects with differences present before treatment.

Read a little further

This is one pattern of confounding by treatment selection. In other settings, the sickest patients may receive the more intensive option, so the direction of bias can reverse. A preference predicts confounding only if it is also related to the potential outcome, or is a proxy for such a cause.

Adjustment relies on sufficient recorded baseline information and suitable methods. In chapter 4, open Data & settings and change Hidden confounding to see what happens when a shared cause of treatment choice and survival is omitted.

Read at a meaningful horizon

Ask about survival at a follow-up time that matters for the clinical question. Read the estimated difference and its uncertainty together; a peak in this curve is not a time to stop or switch treatment.

Read a little further

A single proportional-hazards summary can obscure changes over follow-up. Crossing survival curves do not force the fitted hazard ratio to equal one. This demo also shows survival differences standardized from a Cox model; its constant treatment coefficient cannot represent a reversal.

Choose the principal horizon before inspecting the results, and show the surrounding trajectory. The displayed intervals are pointwise, not a simultaneous statement about the whole curve. These are simulated outcomes, with no real regimen being compared.

An average is not a personal forecast

Even an average for patients with similar baseline characteristics cannot tell us whether this particular person benefits.

Read a little further

Prespecify subgroup comparisons intended to support confirmatory claims. Exploratory heterogeneity analyses are also possible, with suitable separation of discovery and evaluation, uncertainty assessment and validation. This demo does not export clinical subgroup treatment effects.

What must the record support?

Were key common causes of treatment choice and survival recorded before treatment? Were both options possible for comparable patients? Is follow-up adequate at the horizon we want to understand?

Read a little further

Censoring may depend on recorded prognosis. What the analysis needs is a defensible assumption that, within treatment and the covariates used for adjustment, censoring carries no further information about survival, together with enough chance of remaining observed through the horizon.

Also define the treatment plans, align eligibility and treatment assignment with time zero, and specify the population. An estimator cannot create missing treatment support or resolve unmeasured confounding without additional information or assumptions.

If one of the three questions in the last reading has the answer “no”, a more flexible estimator will not repair it. That is the order of operations this walkthrough keeps returning to: the question and the record first, the estimator second.

Chapter 4

Where the method sits.

This chapter is the depth behind chapters 2 and 3: the comparison that makes the question causal, the simulated study with its controls, how benefit can differ across people, what changes when treatment itself changes over time, and the roadmap that puts the question before the estimator. Each part stands on its own. Take what you need.

The groups were different before treatment.

In the starting example, healthier people are more likely to receive treatment. They also tend to survive longer. A direct comparison can mistake some of that advantage for a treatment effect.

We need to compare survival under both options for the same population. Adjusting for recorded differences helps address this problem. But even a well-adjusted comparison can impose the wrong shape on the answer.

Adjustment and overlap in the repository ↗

An early advantage can disappear.

In the starting example below, treatment improves survival early on. Later, the true survival difference reverses. A Cox model with one constant treatment coefficient cannot describe both parts of that story.

A causal survival forest (CSF) takes a different route: estimate the survival difference at each follow-up time, adjusting for recorded baseline characteristics and accounting for censoring. It does not require a constant treatment hazard ratio.

CAST connects those estimates with a curve, accounting for their shared uncertainty. The time pattern becomes easier to see, but a smooth curve can still miss features of the truth.

What proportional hazards assumes

A proportional-hazards model with one constant treatment coefficient assumes a constant treatment hazard ratio. It can still produce a survival-probability difference that changes with time. What this fitted specification cannot reproduce is the reversal shown here: the same population’s survival under A starts above B and ends below it. Cox models with time-varying treatment effects can relax that restriction.

Check the intervals and the smooth band separately.

Good coverage for CSF estimates at individual horizons does not guarantee good coverage for the smoothed CAST trajectory. In a separate study of 300 simulated 600-person cohorts, the nominal 95% intervals contained each cohort's true average effect at 36 months this often:

These percentages describe coverage across repeated simulated studies, not the fraction of people who benefit. This comparison uses the reversal scenario, moderate measured confounding and no hidden confounding; it is separate from the chart controls above. Treatment assignment is logistic by construction, so the results do not establish a generally best propensity model.

CAST adds a quadratic approximation to the CSF estimates. A precise smooth fit can still miss a nonquadratic effect curve. Its band is pointwise, not simultaneous across time, and does not fully account for approximation or model-selection uncertainty.

How does benefit vary across people?

The cohort average can obscure differences between groups. A dose that helps one group may help another less, and those dose–response curves can change over time.

Hector and Bob are two profiles, not one average. A plan that helps Hector early may help Bob less, or later, and the gap between them can itself change over follow-up. The invented curves below show two such profiles responding to a dose over time.

The curves and units are invented for teaching and have no clinical interpretation. The CAST results above describe a cohort-average comparison of treatment with control; dose-response and subgroup effects would require additional analyses.

A fuller analysis would estimate how specified doses affect outcomes over time for people with different baseline profiles. It would need suitable data, clearly defined contrasts and uncertainty estimates. These would still be profile averages: Hector’s individual counterfactual would remain unknown.

State the target One dose at baseline, one profile, one horizon

τ(a, t, x) = E[Yᵃ(t) − Y⁰(t) | X = x]

Here a is a specified baseline dose, t is follow-up time, and x describes baseline characteristics. This motivating target compares dose a with zero dose on the same outcome scale. A sequence of doses chosen as health changes defines a different intervention, returning us to the longitudinal-policy question.

What if treatment itself changes during follow-up?

What changes over time?

Longitudinal analysis follows people through time. It can describe changing outcomes; causal longitudinal analysis asks how those outcomes would change under different treatment choices or histories.

We might compare one decision at the start and follow its effects over time, or study decisions that change during follow-up in response to a person’s history.

Related approaches Where TMLE, LTMLE, LMTP and CAST meet

These approaches contribute different parts of a causal analysis, from defining an intervention to estimating and summarizing its effects. Work on intervention policies and effect trajectories builds on established estimation frameworks.

TMLEAn estimation framework
Targeted maximum likelihood (or minimum loss-based) estimation updates an initial estimate toward a specified target. It can combine flexible prediction with causal estimation under appropriate assumptions.
LTMLELongitudinal estimation
Applies targeted estimation through an ordered history of measurements, treatments and censoring. It can estimate outcomes under specified treatment regimes, including rules that respond to evolving covariates.
LMTPA class of intervention policies
Longitudinal modified treatment policies can modify a naturally occurring treatment value using the available history, such as a specified dose reduction where supported. Their effects can be estimated using TMLE or sequentially doubly robust estimators.
CSF → CASTThe survival branch explored here
Causal survival forests supply effect estimates at chosen horizons. CAST models their trajectory. This demonstration follows a binary baseline decision and displays cohort averages.

TMLE-based methods can also address baseline treatments and survival outcomes. For repeated decisions, treatment–confounder feedback may also need to be addressed. The choice of estimator depends on the target and assumptions about treatment assignment, support and censoring.

Our place on the map

Causal question → survival contrast → causal survival forests → CAST trajectory

Start by specifying the causal target and assumptions, then choose estimation tools. Target-trial emulation provides a design framework; prediction and simulation can support an analysis within that design. Causal identification still depends on the data and assumptions.

Can these data answer it?

Before choosing an estimator, specify the question, the available data and the assumptions connecting them. Identification asks whether the causal quantity can be expressed in terms of the observed data distribution.

A causal diagram can express part of that contract. We also need well-defined treatments, relevant treatment support, assumptions about confounding and censoring, and enough information for useful precision.

The causal roadmap organizes these steps, from defining the question through identification, estimation and interpretation. CSF and CAST contribute to the estimation step.

Chapter 6

Where this leaves you.

  1. An observed survival gap mixes two things: the effect of a treatment and the head start of the people who received it. Name the second before reading the first.
  2. Adjustment removes the part of that head start the record captured, and only that part. A factor nobody wrote down pulls every method off the truth in the same direction while the usual warning signs look healthy.
  3. A single hazard ratio averages over follow-up. When an early benefit becomes a late harm it is wrong at every time point. Read the difference at the horizon that matters, in survivors per 100.
  4. A smooth trajectory is easier to read and can still miss the truth. CAST’s band is pointwise, and in the reversal example it misses at 84 months. Check the per-horizon intervals and the band separately.
  5. The question comes before the estimator. What was recorded, whether both options were possible for comparable patients, whether follow-up reaches the horizon. If the answer is no, a better estimator does not repair it.

What this walkthrough does not establish

Every patient is simulated and the two options are abstract, so nothing here is evidence about any actual regimen, drug or dose. No method is crowned: each cohort is one random draw, and the propensity comparison used a logistic treatment generator that favors the simpler models. Nothing is validated on patient data.
