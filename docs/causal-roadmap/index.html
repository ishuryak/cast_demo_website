Causal roadmap primer · for medical students and residents

From a clinical question to a causal answer

You have a clinical question, a dataset, and some programming skills. You know Cox regression, random forests and XGBoost. This page shows a different starting point: decide which question you are asking, design the study you wish you could run, and only then choose an estimator. The running example compares hypofractionated with conventionally fractionated radiotherapy.

The goal

The ultimate goal is better treatment: choosing the schedule that works best for each group of patients and, as far as the data allow, for each patient given their characteristics. A retrospective causal analysis estimates how much a treatment choice changes outcomes, on average and in subgroups. On the way there, the end goal of the analysis itself is an argument about whether a new randomized controlled trial (RCT) of the question would be useful (section 10). The same methods also work on RCT data (section 9).

Background

Where these ideas come from

Modern causal inference grew from three strands that are now used together: potential outcomes (what would happen to the same patient under each treatment), causal diagrams (what causes what), and estimation methods that let machine learning do the modeling without breaking the statistics.

A short timeline
  • 1923. Neyman applied counterfactual (potential) outcomes to the estimation of causal effects in randomized experiments.1
  • 1974 to 1983. Rubin extended Neyman's framework to observational studies.1 Rosenbaum and Rubin defined the propensity score, the probability of treatment given baseline covariates.2
  • 1986. Robins generalized the framework to treatments that change over time and introduced the g-formula, the root of the g-methods used for treatment policies (section 6).1
  • 1988 to 2009. Pearl formalized causal diagrams: d-separation, and the backdoor criterion that tells you which variables to adjust for (section 3).1 Later, Bareinboim and Pearl worked on fusing data from different populations and designs, including transporting results from one population to another.3
  • 2000s to 2010s. Van der Laan and colleagues developed targeted learning, including targeted maximum likelihood estimation, and with Petersen described the causal roadmap this page follows.4
  • 2016. Hernán and Robins described target trial emulation: write the randomized trial you would run, then make the observational analysis follow its protocol.5
  • 2018. Chernozhukov and colleagues introduced double/debiased machine learning,6 and Wager and Athey introduced causal forests for treatment effects that vary between patients.7
Some of the people behind these ideas, with links

A short list of researchers whose work this page draws on. It is not exhaustive: many others shaped the field.

  • Judea Pearl (UCLA): causal diagrams and the do-calculus.
  • Donald Rubin (Harvard, emeritus, and Temple): potential outcomes for observational studies, propensity scores.
  • James Robins (Harvard): g-methods for treatments that change over time.
  • Miguel Hernán (Harvard): target trial emulation, and the free book Causal Inference: What If with Robins.
  • Mark van der Laan (Berkeley): targeted learning and super learning.
  • Elias Bareinboim (Columbia): causal artificial intelligence, data fusion and transportability.
  • Victor Chernozhukov (MIT): double/debiased machine learning.
  • Susan Athey and Stefan Wager (Stanford): causal forests and machine learning for treatment effects.

0 · Before anything else

Three questions that look alike

The same registry can answer three different kinds of question: description, prediction, and cause and effect. Each needs different tools.8

The core difference

A causal effect compares two outcomes for the same patients: one under hypofractionation and one under conventional fractionation. Each patient shows us only one of them. No cohort size and no better learner can fill in the other, so ordinary prediction accuracy cannot confirm a causal effect. A causal analysis is built on a design and on stated assumptions, and then challenged from several directions.

Why a prediction model is not a causal answer

Prediction models are useful, and nothing on this page argues against them. The problem starts when a prediction model is asked a cause-and-effect question. A survival model with fractionation as one of its inputs will give fractionation a coefficient, a hazard ratio or a SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) value. That number describes the model. It is the effect of hypofractionation only under conditions that the model never checks: the right variables adjusted for, none adjusted for that should not be, and comparable patients in both groups. Section 7 shows three simulated cases where the model that predicts better reports the wrong effect.

Not only for registry data

Causal machine learning is often presented as a way to analyze registry data, and most of this page uses registry data. The same methods also work on RCT data, with different assumptions: randomization settles confounding of the assigned treatment, and the methods then add precision, show who benefits, and carry trial results to other patients. Section 9 explains what they add there.

Try it: which kind of question is this?

    1 · The question and the data

    Start with the clinical question, then find data that can answer it

    The causal roadmap is a sequence of steps from a scientific question to an estimate and its interpretation.4,9 The first step is to write the question so precisely that two people would compute the same number from it: the population, the strategies compared, the outcome, the time horizon and the scale.

    The five elements, for the running example

    A vague version is “Is hypofractionation better?”. A precise version names five things:

    ElementRunning example
    PopulationAdults starting curative-intent radiotherapy for a given tumor site, for whom both schedules are clinically reasonable
    Strategies comparedModerate hypofractionation versus conventional fractionation (about 1.8 to 2 Gy per fraction), each fully specified at the start: total dose, dose per fraction, number of fractions, and any concurrent therapy. In a real protocol these are filled in for the tumor site. Two analysts must not be able to read the same words as two different treatments.
    OutcomeDeath from any cause
    Time horizon5 years from the first fraction
    ScaleDifference in restricted mean survival time (RMST) at 5 years, in months, plus the differences in 2- and 5-year survival probability
    Why ask this with observational data at all?

    For some tumor sites, randomized trials have already compared the two approaches. In prostate cancer, for example, the CHHiP trial found 60 Gy in 20 fractions noninferior to 74 Gy in 37 fractions for its primary endpoint, biochemical or clinical failure.10 Trials enroll selected patients, though. Registries and institutional records include older and frailer patients, other tumor sites, and later years. Consequently, a well-designed observational analysis can ask whether a trial result carries over to the patients a trial left out, or address a site where no trial exists. It cannot replace a trial where one is feasible. Often its most useful product is a well-founded argument for or against the next trial (section 10).

    Which data, and what they lack

    Before any modeling, list what the question needs and check whether the data have it:

    • The treatment, defined at the start. The prescribed dose per fraction, number of fractions and total dose, not only the total.
    • Time zero and follow-up. The date of the first fraction, the date of death or last contact, and why follow-up ended.
    • Everything that drove the choice of schedule and also affects survival. Age, comorbidity, stage, tumor size, performance status, distance to the treating center, facility, insurance, calendar year.
    • The outcome. Death from any cause is usually recorded well. Recurrence, toxicity and cause of death often are not.

    National registries such as the National Cancer Database (NCDB) and Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) are large but may lack performance status, toxicity and recurrence. An institutional electronic health record (EHR) may have them but covers fewer patients. Write down the gaps now. They become the sensitivity analyses of section 8.

    2 · The target trial

    Write the trial you would run, then imitate it

    Target trial emulation means writing the protocol of the randomized trial that would answer your question, and then making the observational analysis follow that protocol as closely as the data allow.5 It removes several biases that no estimator can fix later, and the protocol becomes the draft of a real trial if one is needed (section 10).

    The protocol, element by element
    Protocol elementThe target trialThe emulation in registry or EHR data

    StrategiesConventional versus moderate hypofractionation, fully specified, as prescribedClassify by the prescribed schedule at the start, not by what was eventually delivered
    AssignmentRandomNot random. Assume the groups are comparable after adjusting for the measured baseline factors (section 5).
    Time zeroRandomization, immediately before the first fractionThe first day of radiotherapy, for both groups

    Causal contrastEffect of being assigned to a schedule (intention to treat), and of following it (per protocol)Usually the analog of intention to treat. A per-protocol analog needs data on interruptions and switching.
    Analysis planCompare survival between randomized armsAdjusted estimator chosen in section 7, prespecified, with the checks of section 8
    Trap: starting the clock late, or grouping by the course completed

    A conventional course takes several weeks longer than a hypofractionated one. Two different mistakes can follow, and both come from misaligning eligibility, assignment and the start of follow-up.11

    • Starting follow-up at the end of radiotherapy. Deaths during treatment are lost, so each group is restricted to patients who survived to a later start, and that start is later for the conventional group. This is a selection bias: it changes who is being compared. Its direction is not fixed. It depends on how the risk of death changes over those weeks, so it can favor either schedule.
    • Classifying patients by the course they completed. To be counted as having completed the longer course, a patient must survive until it ends. That stretch of guaranteed survival is immortal time, and it makes completers look better than they are.

    Starting the clock at the first fraction and classifying by the prescription avoids both. A landmark analysis that starts later on purpose is not wrong in itself, but it answers a different question about a different population, the patients who survived to the landmark.

    Trap: classifying by the dose received

    A patient who becomes too sick to continue receives fewer fractions than prescribed. Classifying by delivered fractions moves the sickest patients into whichever group looks like a shorter course. The prescribed schedule is the treatment strategy. What happened during the course is an outcome of that strategy.

    3 · The causal model

    Draw what causes what before choosing what to adjust for

    A directed acyclic graph (DAG) is a diagram where an arrow means “affects”. It decides which variables belong in the model, and that decision matters more than the choice between Cox, a random forest and a neural network.

    Three rules

    Adjust for common causes measured before treatment. Do not adjust for anything the treatment itself affects when estimating the total effect of the schedule chosen at the start. Name what is missing.

    The causal diagram for the running example
    The three rules, explained
    • Adjust for common causes measured before treatment. Age, comorbidity, stage, facility and calendar year can each push toward one schedule and also change survival. If uptake of hypofractionation changed over the years while other care also improved, calendar year is a confounder. These factors do drive the choice in practice: in a US registry study of breast cancer, older patients were much more likely to receive hypofractionation, and so were patients living 50 miles or more from the treating facility.12
    • Do not adjust for anything the treatment itself affects, in a baseline analysis of the total effect. Treatment interruptions, acute toxicity, early tumor response and later systemic therapy lie on the path from schedule to survival. Entering them as ordinary covariates removes part of the effect and can add new bias.13 A prediction workflow puts them in because they raise accuracy. A causal workflow for the total effect leaves them out for exactly that reason. Methods for per-protocol effects, treatment rules and censoring (sections 6 and 9) do use post-treatment history, on purpose and with g-methods. That is a different operation from adding it to a regression.
    • Name what is missing. If frailer patients with poor performance status are steered toward the shorter course, and performance status is not recorded, the comparison is confounded by indication. No estimator fixes this. Section 8 asks how strong it would have to be to change the conclusion.

    4 · The estimand

    Decide the exact number you want

    An estimand is the quantity your analysis is supposed to estimate, written before any data are touched. For the running example: the average 5-year RMST if every eligible patient received hypofractionation, minus the same quantity if every eligible patient received conventional fractionation.

    What RMST is, and why it suits clinical decisions

    RMST is the area under the survival curve up to 5 years, in other words the average number of months lived within those 5 years.14 A difference of 2 months is something a patient and a clinician can weigh against convenience and toxicity.

    For whom: the whole population, the treated, or subgroups?
    • Average treatment effect (ATE): everyone eligible. Answers “what if we changed practice for this whole population?”.
    • Average treatment effect in the treated (ATT): the patients who actually received hypofractionation. Answers “did it help the patients we gave it to?”.
    • Conditional average treatment effect (CATE): patients with given characteristics, such as age 75 or older. Answers “who benefits more?”, the question behind tailoring treatment to subgroups and individual patients. It is harder to estimate reliably and needs its own checks.
    Why not a hazard ratio?

    The hazard ratio (HR) from a Cox model compares death rates among patients still alive at each moment. If the treatment helps early, the patients still alive later in the two groups differ in more than their treatment, so a hazard ratio has selection built into it. It also changes over time whenever hazards are not proportional, and a single HR then averages over an effect that is moving.15 A Cox model remains a good description tool and a reasonable outcome model inside some causal estimators. However, the number to report for a causal question is usually a difference in survival probability or RMST at stated times.

    Go deeper on survival effects

    How an effect can rise, peak and even reverse over follow-up, why one hazard ratio cannot show that, and how a causal survival forest and CAST estimate the whole trajectory are worked through with simulated data on the CAST walkthrough. The methods and diagnostics page lets you change the strength of confounding and watch each method respond.

    5 · Identification

    Can these data answer the question at all?

    Identification asks whether the estimand could be computed from the observed data if you had unlimited patients. For the treatment, it rests on three assumptions: exchangeability, positivity and consistency.1 For a survival outcome, it also rests on assumptions about censoring.16

    Two layers

    Identification decides whether the data can answer the question. Estimation (section 7) decides how well you compute the answer. Better models, including neural networks, help the second layer. They cannot fix a failure of the first.

    The three treatment assumptions, in plain clinical terms

    Positivity (overlap)Every kind of patient could plausibly have received either schedule.     Censoring and follow-up: the assumptions survival adds

    Patients who are lost to follow-up or reach the data cutoff are censored. Two more conditions are then needed:16

    • Ignorable censoring. Given the measured variables, whether a patient is censored is unrelated to how long they would have survived. Administrative censoring at a fixed data cutoff usually satisfies this. Loss to follow-up in sicker or healthier patients may not.
    • Follow-up through the horizon. Every kind of patient needs a real chance of still being followed at 5 years. A group that is all censored by year 3 cannot support a 5-year estimate, however good the model.

    Randomization does not fix informative loss to follow-up: it balances the assigned treatment, not who drops out later. Many estimators exist in a complete-outcome form and a survival form. The plan must name a version that handles censoring, such as censoring weights or a survival-specific estimator.

    6 · Treatment policies

    Compare the rules clinicians actually use

    “Everyone gets hypofractionation” versus “everyone gets conventional fractionation” is a static comparison. Clinicians follow rules, and a causal analysis can compare rules directly: dynamic rules that depend on the patient, and modified treatment policies that shift practice a step.

    Four kinds of strategy, with examples
    Kind of strategyExampleQuestion it answers
    StaticAll eligible patients receive hypofractionationWhat if practice switched completely?
    Dynamic, decided onceHypofractionation if age is 70 or older or travel time exceeds one hour, conventional otherwise, decided at the startIs this triage rule better than current practice, or than treating everyone the same way?
    Modified treatment policyA fully specified change to each patient's own prescription, for example: the same total dose in five fewer fractions, with fraction size raised to match, applied only where dose constraints allow and where such schedules were actually usedWhat if we shifted practice a step, instead of all the way?
    Dynamic, during treatmentReplan the course if weight loss during treatment exceeds 10%Decisions made during treatment, based on how the patient is doing

    A dynamic strategy is a rule that assigns treatment at each decision point from the patient's history up to that point.1 A rule decided once from baseline factors needs only single-time-point methods. Longitudinal modified treatment policies (LMTP) define the effect of changing each patient's treatment as a function of the treatment the patient would otherwise have received and of their history, with treatment and confounders that change over time.17 A modified policy is a hypothetical, fully specified map from the observed prescription to a new one. It is something to estimate, not an instruction to remove fractions.

    Trap: time-varying confounding

    Weight loss during treatment can be caused by the earlier fractions and can also trigger a change of plan. When earlier treatment affects a factor that then drives later treatment, traditional adjustment, such as entering weight loss as a time-varying covariate in a Cox model, can introduce bias even when every confounder is measured.1 The g-methods, which include the LMTP estimators, are built for this setting.17

    7 · The estimator

    Choose the estimator last, and put your machine learning inside it

    Once the estimand is identified, several estimators can compute it. Most need two prediction tasks, called nuisance models: the outcome given baseline factors, and the probability of receiving each schedule given baseline factors (the propensity score).2 This is where random forests, XGBoost and neural networks go. They stop being the analysis and become components of it.

    The estimator menu

    What “doubly robust” does and does not promise

    “Doubly robust” means the estimate stays consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity model is correct, given the identification assumptions of section 5.1 Its larger practical benefit is that flexible machine learning can be used for both. The confidence interval keeps its meaning only under further conditions: overlap, nuisance models learned well enough, an appropriate score for the estimand, and cross-fitting or similar safeguards.6 A poorly fitted learner does not earn a valid interval. Running two or three estimators that depend on different models is also one of the checks in section 8.

    Why this happens

    The covariates that mostly predict who receives hypofractionation add little to predicting the outcome, so the penalty shrinks them first. Shrinking them hands their share of the confounding to the schedule term. Prediction accuracy barely changes, so a model chosen for accuracy cannot see the problem. Double machine learning uses the lasso twice, once to predict the outcome and once to predict the schedule, each on held-out folds, and then relates the two sets of residuals. Errors in either prediction then affect the effect estimate only at second order. Because the simulated effect is the same for every patient, this simple residual-on-residual version targets it. With effects that vary, it would target a weighted average instead. Unpenalized least squares would also work here, because the model is linear and the sample is large. The lesson is not to read an effect off a model tuned for prediction.

    8 · Checks and sensitivity

    Build confidence without an answer key

    A prediction is validated on held-out outcomes. A causal estimate cannot be validated that way, so confidence comes from how much it withstands: overlap, agreement across estimators, planted-effect and negative-control checks, and a bias analysis for what was not measured.

    A practical sequence of checks
    1. Overlap and balance. Plot the propensity scores by group, and check that the adjusted groups are similar on every baseline factor.
    2. Agreement across estimators. If TMLE, IPTW and a causal survival forest, which rely on different models, give similar answers, the result does not hinge on one modeling choice.
    3. A known truth is recovered. Simulate data with a planted effect that resembles your cohort, run the same pipeline, and confirm it returns the planted effect.
    4. Software and bias checks that should give nothing. Shuffling the treatment labels is a software check: the estimate should be near zero. A negative-control outcome, one the treatment cannot affect, is a check on bias: a clearly nonzero estimate signals residual confounding to investigate, although it does not tell you the size or direction of the bias in the main result.
    5. How wrong could it be? State a plausible range of unmeasured confounding, with reasons, and ask whether the estimate stays on the same side of your clinical thresholds for benefit and harm across that range. For an RMST difference, do this on the RMST scale, with a bias model or a simulation that adds the hidden factor. The E-value expresses the needed strength of confounding on the risk ratio scale. It applies directly to a risk ratio, such as for 5-year mortality, or to a ratio of mean survival times. A difference in months needs a conversion whose assumptions must be stated, so a bias analysis on the RMST scale is usually clearer.19 For regression-based estimates, another approach expresses it as the share of variation in treatment and outcome a hidden factor would need, compared against measured covariates such as age or stage.20

    None of these proves the answer is right. Together they show which conclusions survive and which depend on a single assumption. The CAST methods page shows the bias question for survival data: a simulated unmeasured confounder moves every method at once while the visible diagnostics still look healthy.

    Summary

    What each approach tells you about the same question

    A Cox model with a treatment term gives one association, a prediction model forecasts outcomes, and a causal roadmap estimates what would happen under each strategy. Only the last answers a treatment decision directly.

    Habits worth changing
    • Putting every available variable into the model. Adjust for baseline common causes from the DAG, and leave out what the treatment affects.

    • Reporting a hazard ratio as “the effect”. Report survival or RMST differences at prespecified times.
    • Starting follow-up at the end of treatment. Start it where the target trial would randomize.
    • Choosing the model with the best C-index for an effect question. Choose the estimator for the estimand, then check it as in section 8.

    9 · Beyond registries

    Causal machine learning on randomized trial data

    The same estimators apply to observational and randomized studies alike. What changes is which assumptions the design guarantees. Randomization makes the assigned groups comparable, so the effect of being assigned to a schedule, the intention-to-treat effect, needs no adjustment for confounding.1 The propensity score is known: it is the randomization probability. Censoring and nonadherence are not settled by randomization.

    Covariate adjustmentThe same average effect, more precisely. Adjusting for baseline factors that predict the outcome generally narrows the confidence interval and makes tests more powerful, with minimal impact on bias or the type I error rate.21 AIPW and TMLE with machine-learning outcome models are one way to do this.Prespecify the covariates and the method, and do not adjust for covariates measured after randomization, which the treatment could affect.21 The gain is expected, not guaranteed for every trial. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) guidance on adjustment does not cover machine-learning methods, so a registration trial needs to check current regulatory views.
    Who benefitsHow the effect varies with baseline factors (the CATE), from a causal forest or causal survival forest.7,16 Randomization removes confounding from these estimates. What remains is a statistical problem.Trials are powered for the average effect. The Predictive Approaches to Treatment effect Heterogeneity (PATH) statement recommends analyses by baseline risk first. It warns that flexible effect models can produce signals of benefit and harm even for a treatment with no effect, and asks for results on both absolute and relative scales, with emphasis on absolute risk differences.22 Treat subgroups as hypotheses.
    Effects over timeHow a trial's effect rises, peaks or fades over follow-up, from causal survival forest estimates at several horizons joined by CAST,18 instead of one hazard ratioPrespecify the horizons. Late horizons rest on the few patients still under follow-up, and censoring must be ignorable.
    Per-protocol effectWhat would have happened if everyone had completed the assigned schedule, rather than the effect of being assigned to itAdherence is not randomized. Patients who stop a course early differ from those who finish it, so this effect needs g-methods and data on the factors that changed during treatment.1
    Extending the result to other patientsThe effect in patients the trial left out, such as older or frailer patients, by combining the trial with registry data. This is called generalizability when the trial population is a subset of the target population, and transportability when the trial population lies at least partly outside it.23The factors that modify the effect and differ between the two populations must be measured in both, and every kind of target patient needs some chance of having been in the trial. Patients excluded outright by the eligibility criteria had no such chance, so an estimate for them is an extrapolation that rests on modeling assumptions.23
    Same tools, different hard parts

    With registry data, the hard part is identification (section 5). With trial data, identification of the intention-to-treat effect comes with the design, apart from censoring. The hard parts become precision, heterogeneity in a sample powered only for the average, adherence, and the gap between trial patients and the patients you treat.

    10 · The end goal

    Does the evidence justify a new randomized trial?

    The most useful product of a retrospective causal analysis is often a clear, conditional argument about the next trial: whether one is worth considering, for which patients, comparing which fully specified strategies, on which endpoint, and judged by superiority or noninferiority. The target trial of section 2 is already a draft protocol.

    Honest in both directions

    When a randomized trial is feasible, randomization still generally gives more certainty that an effect is causal than an analysis of nonrandomized data. When the identification assumptions are highly implausible and the available data cannot improve them, the causal roadmap's own advice is to collect new data prospectively.9 The analysis informs the decision. Feasibility, cost, equipoise and patient priorities complete it.

    Two lessons from history

    Hormone therapy. Observational studies had suggested that postmenopausal hormone therapy lowered the risk of coronary heart disease. The Women's Health Initiative randomized trial then found a higher risk in women assigned to estrogen plus progestin. When the observational Nurses' Health Study was reanalyzed as an emulation of that trial, comparing women who started therapy with women who did not, its intention-to-treat estimates were similar to the trial's. The remaining discrepancy could be largely explained by differences in time since menopause and in length of follow-up.24 A design-first analysis reproduced the trial, and where the two differed, the reason pointed to the next question.

    Dose escalation in lung cancer. Phase 1 and 2 trials suggested that 74 Gy with concurrent chemotherapy was tolerable in stage III non-small cell lung cancer and implied a survival benefit. The randomized RTOG 0617 trial found the opposite: median overall survival was 28.7 months with 60 Gy and 20.3 months with 74 Gy.25 Encouraging nonrandomized evidence is a reason to test, not a substitute for testing.

    From your results to an argument

    First set your thresholds: the smallest benefit and the smallest harm, on the primary estimand, that would change practice. For a noninferiority question, the harm threshold is the noninferiority margin. Then read the interval and the bias analysis against them.

    If your analysis showsIt argues
    A precise, credible trial already answered the same question in patients like yours, and your emulation reproduces itA duplicate trial of that question is likely a low priority. An imprecise trial, or one with a different endpoint or horizon, does not settle it.
    An interval that includes a meaningful benefit and a meaningful harm, or benefit and no effectDecision-relevant uncertainty. A trial is worth considering, with a sample size planned from a clinically relevant target, not from this interval alone.
    A clear benefit that plausible unmeasured confounding could move across a thresholdOnly randomization can settle it. Consider a trial, and name the factor you could not measure.
    A clear harm that plausible unmeasured confounding could move across a thresholdInconclusive. A superiority trial of the new schedule is hard to justify. Examine the harm signal first.
    A clear benefit that survives the bias analysis and reproduces existing trialsA strong hypothesis for a confirmatory or pragmatic trial. Whether one is still needed depends on whether clinicians remain uncertain.
    A meaningful harm possible, meaningful benefit excluded, across the bias rangeA superiority trial is likely a low priority. A noninferiority question would first have to rule out the harm.
    A clear harm that survives the bias analysisA harm signal. Randomizing patients to that schedule may no longer be acceptable. Examine the finding before proposing a trial.
    Effects too small to matter, across the plausible bias range, for a superiority questionA superiority trial on that endpoint is likely a low priority. It does not show the schedules are equivalent for other outcomes.
    Similar outcomes, for a shorter schedule with other advantagesCompatible with noninferiority, which only a randomized trial with a prespecified endpoint and margin can establish.
    Patient groups that almost always received one scheduleThe estimate applies only where there is overlap (section 5). A trial in the other patients is a separate question.
    An effect that seems to differ between prespecified groupsA hypothesis for stratified randomization or an enriched trial, not a finding.22
    Try it: build the argument from your results
    What is the new schedule meant to achieve?
    Has a randomized trial already compared these strategies?
    Did your emulation reproduce a related trial result?
    Where does the 95% interval lie, against your benefit and harm thresholds? (For noninferiority, the harm threshold is the margin.)
    Across a plausible range of unmeasured confounding, would your reading of the interval above stay the same?
    Does the overlap cover your whole target population?
    Did the effect seem to differ between prespecified groups?
    What the analysis hands to the trial
    • The protocol. Eligibility, fully specified strategies, time zero and follow-up come from the target trial of section 2.
    • The primary endpoint. For example, an RMST difference at a prespecified horizon. Royston and Parmar proposed RMST as an alternative to the hazard ratio for designing as well as analyzing trials with a time-to-event outcome.14 A noninferiority trial also needs a prespecified, clinically justified margin.
    • The sample size. From a clinically relevant target effect or margin, with scenario assumptions for control-arm survival, accrual, censoring and feasibility. Your interval reflects sampling error only, not bias, so it is one input, not the plan.
    • Stratification and adjustment. The strongest prognostic factors, any prespecified effect modifier, and a prespecified covariate-adjusted analysis (section 9).
    • The patients at greatest uncertainty. Groups an earlier trial excluded, and groups without overlap, each as its own stated question.
    • The case itself. The unmeasured factors from section 8 are reasons a trial is needed, and baseline variables it should collect.
    The one-paragraph argument

    “In [population], [strategy B, fully specified] compared with [strategy A] changed 5-year RMST by [estimate] months (95% interval [lower to upper]), against prespecified thresholds of [benefit] and [harm]. Across a plausible range of unmeasured confounding [stated with reasons], the conclusion [does / does not] hold, and the emulation [does / does not] reproduce [existing trial]. A randomized [superiority / noninferiority] trial is therefore [worth considering / a low priority] in [patients], with [endpoint and margin], a sample size planned from [target], stratified by [factors].” The worksheet below ends with this paragraph.
