Causal roadmap primer · for medical students and residents

From a clinical question to a causal answer

You have a clinical question, a dataset, and some programming skills. You know Cox regression, random forests and XGBoost, and perhaps you are wondering whether a neural network would do better. This page shows a different starting point: decide which question you are asking, design the study you wish you could run, and only then choose an estimator. The running example compares hypofractionated with conventionally fractionated radiotherapy.

Igor Shuryak, MD, PhD · is144@cumc.columbia.edu · Center for Radiological Research, Columbia University Irving Medical Center

Companion to the Causal Analysis for Survival Trajectories (CAST) demo by Andy Wilson, Aimee Harrison and Igor Shuryak. This page covers the general causal roadmap. Where the question becomes specifically about survival over time, it hands you over to that site. All examples here are simulated or come from published trials. No patient data are used.

0 · Before anything else

Three questions that look alike

The same registry can answer three different kinds of question, and each needs different tools.1

Kind of questionExample on hypofractionationTypical toolsHow you know the answer is right
DescriptionHow did the share of hypofractionated courses change between 2010 and 2022, by age group?Counts, proportions, Kaplan-Meier curves by groupCheck the counts against the source
PredictionWhich patients starting radiotherapy will die within 3 years?Cox regression, random survival forest, XGBoost, neural networksHeld-out data: concordance index (C-index), calibration. The outcome is eventually observed, so you can score it.
Cause and effectWould patients aged 75 or older live longer, on average, if they all received hypofractionation instead of conventional fractionation?Target trial emulation plus a causal estimator (this page)No held-out score exists. The outcome under the schedule a patient did not receive is never observed for anyone.

Prediction models are useful, and nothing on this page argues against them. The problem starts when a prediction model is asked a cause-and-effect question. A survival model with fractionation as one of its inputs will give fractionation a coefficient, a hazard ratio or a SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) value. That number describes the model. It is the effect of hypofractionation only under conditions that the model never checks: the right variables adjusted for, none adjusted for that should not be, and comparable patients in both groups. Section 7 shows three simulated cases where the model that predicts better reports the wrong effect.

The core difference

A causal effect compares two outcomes for the same patients: one under hypofractionation and one under conventional fractionation. Each patient shows us only one of them. No cohort size and no better learner can fill in the other, so a causal analysis cannot be checked the way a prediction is. It has to be built on a design and on stated assumptions, and then challenged from several directions.

Try it: which kind of question is this?

    1 · Roadmap step 1

    Start with the clinical question, then find data that can answer it

    The causal roadmap is a sequence of steps that moves from a scientific question to an estimate and its interpretation.2,3 This page follows an adapted version of it. The first step is to write the question so precisely that two people would compute the same number from it.

    A vague version is “Is hypofractionation better?”. A precise version names five things:

    ElementRunning example
    PopulationAdults starting curative-intent radiotherapy for a given tumor site, for whom both schedules are clinically reasonable
    Strategies comparedModerate hypofractionation (larger doses per fraction over fewer fractions) versus conventional fractionation (about 1.8 to 2 Gy per fraction), each defined by the schedule prescribed at the start
    OutcomeDeath from any cause
    Time horizon5 years from the first fraction
    ScaleDifference in restricted mean survival time (RMST) at 5 years, in months, plus the difference in 5-year survival probability

    Why ask this with observational data at all?

    For some tumor sites, randomized trials have already compared the two approaches. In prostate cancer, for example, the CHHiP trial found 60 Gy in 20 fractions non-inferior to 74 Gy in 37 fractions.4 Trials enroll selected patients, though. Registries and institutional records include older and frailer patients, other tumor sites, and later years. Consequently, a well-designed observational analysis can ask whether a trial result carries over to the patients a trial left out, or address a site where no trial exists. It cannot replace a trial where one is feasible.

    Which data, and what they lack

    Before any modeling, list what the question needs and check whether the data have it:

    • The treatment, defined at the start. The prescribed dose per fraction and number of fractions, not only the total dose.
    • Time zero and follow-up. The date of the first fraction, the date of death or last contact.
    • Everything that drove the choice of schedule and also affects survival. Age, comorbidity, stage, tumor size, performance status, distance to the treating center, facility, insurance, calendar year.
    • The outcome. Death from any cause is usually recorded well. Recurrence, toxicity and cause of death often are not.

    National registries such as the National Cancer Database (NCDB) and Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) are large but may lack performance status, toxicity and recurrence. An institutional electronic health record (EHR) may have them but covers fewer patients. Write down the gaps now. They become the sensitivity analyses of step 8.

    2 · Target trial emulation

    Write the trial you would run, then imitate it

    Target trial emulation means writing the protocol of the randomized trial that would answer your question, and then making the observational analysis follow that protocol as closely as the data allow.5 It sounds like paperwork. In practice it removes several biases that no estimator can fix later.

    Protocol elementThe target trialThe emulation in registry or EHR data
    EligibilityAdults with a newly diagnosed tumor of the chosen site, curative intent, no prior radiotherapy to the region, both schedules acceptableThe same criteria from diagnosis codes and treatment fields. Exclude palliative courses and schedules that fit neither strategy.
    StrategiesConventional versus moderate hypofractionation, as prescribedClassify by the prescribed schedule at the start, not by what was eventually delivered
    AssignmentRandomNot random. Assume the groups are comparable after adjusting for the measured baseline factors (step 5).
    Time zeroRandomization, immediately before the first fractionThe first day of radiotherapy, for both groups
    Follow-upFrom time zero to death, loss to follow-up, or 5 yearsThe same, with censoring at last contact
    OutcomeDeath from any causeVital status and date of death
    Causal contrastEffect of being assigned to a schedule (intention to treat), and of following it (per protocol)Usually the analog of intention to treat. A per-protocol analog needs data on interruptions and switching.
    Analysis planCompare survival between randomized armsAdjusted estimator chosen in step 7, prespecified, with the checks of step 8
    Trap: time zero and immortal time

    A conventional course takes several weeks longer than a hypofractionated one. Suppose follow-up starts at the end of radiotherapy, or patients are classified by the course they completed. Then a patient in the conventional group has to survive those extra weeks to be counted in it, while early deaths drop out or move to another group. The conventional group looks healthier than it is. This is immortal time bias, and it comes from misaligning eligibility, assignment and the start of follow-up.6 Starting the clock at the first fraction and classifying by the prescription avoids it.

    Trap: classifying by the dose received

    A patient who becomes too sick to continue receives fewer fractions than prescribed. Classifying by delivered fractions moves the sickest patients into whichever group looks like a shorter course. The prescribed schedule is the treatment strategy. What happened during the course is an outcome of that strategy.

    3 · Roadmap step 2: the causal model

    Draw what causes what before choosing what to adjust for

    A directed acyclic graph (DAG) is a diagram where an arrow means “affects”. It is the cheapest tool on this page and the one most often skipped. It decides which variables belong in the model, and that decision matters more than the choice between Cox, a random forest and a neural network.

    Three rules follow from the diagram:

    • Adjust for common causes measured before treatment. Age, comorbidity, stage, facility and calendar year can each push toward one schedule and also change survival. If uptake of hypofractionation changed over the years while other care also improved, calendar year is a confounder. These factors do drive the choice in practice: in a US registry study of breast cancer, older patients were much more likely to receive hypofractionation, and so were patients living 50 miles or more from the treating facility.7
    • Do not adjust for anything the treatment itself affects. Treatment interruptions, acute toxicity, early tumor response and later systemic therapy lie on the path from schedule to survival. Adjusting for them removes part of the effect and can add new bias.8 A prediction workflow puts them in because they raise accuracy. A causal workflow leaves them out for exactly that reason.
    • Name what is missing. If frailer patients with poor performance status are steered toward the shorter course, and performance status is not recorded, the comparison is confounded by indication. No estimator fixes this. Step 8 asks how strong it would have to be to change the conclusion.

    Simulation 1: better prediction, wrong effect

The causal model adjusts for frailty only. The predictive model also adds the 3-month response, because it improves accuracy on held-out patients.

    How this cohort is simulated

    Frailty is a standard normal score. The probability of hypofractionation rises with frailty. The 3-month response equals 1 × schedule − 0.5 × frailty plus noise. Survival in months equals 48 − 12 × frailty + a direct effect of the schedule + 6 × response, plus noise, with no censoring. The total effect of hypofractionation is the direct effect plus 6, and three scenarios set that total to 0, +4 and −4 months. Half the patients are used for fitting and half are held out.     4 · Roadmap step 3: the estimand

    Decide the exact number you want

    An estimand is the quantity your analysis is supposed to estimate, written before any data are touched. For the running example: the average 5-year RMST if every eligible patient received hypofractionation, minus the same quantity if every eligible patient received conventional fractionation. RMST is the area under the survival curve up to 5 years, in other words the average number of months lived within those 5 years.9 A difference of 2 months is something a patient and a clinician can weigh against convenience and toxicity.

    For whom?

    • Average treatment effect (ATE): everyone eligible. Answers “what if we changed practice for this whole population?”.
    • Average treatment effect in the treated (ATT): the patients who actually received hypofractionation. Answers “did it help the patients we gave it to?”.
    • Conditional average treatment effect (CATE): patients with given characteristics, such as age 75 or older. Answers “who benefits more?”. This is harder to estimate reliably and needs its own checks.

    Why not a hazard ratio?

    The hazard ratio (HR) from a Cox model compares death rates among patients still alive at each moment. If the treatment helps early, the patients still alive later in the two groups differ in more than their treatment, so a hazard ratio has selection built into it. It also changes over time whenever hazards are not proportional, and a single HR then averages over an effect that is moving.10 A Cox model remains a good description tool and a reasonable outcome model inside some causal estimators. However, the number to report for a causal question is usually a difference in survival probability or RMST at stated times.

    Go deeper on survival effects

    How an effect can rise, peak and even reverse over follow-up, why one hazard ratio cannot show that, and how a causal survival forest and CAST estimate the whole trajectory are worked through with simulated data on the CAST walkthrough.     5 · Roadmap step 4: identification

    Can these data answer the question at all?

    Identification asks whether the estimand could be computed from the observed data if you had unlimited patients. It depends on three assumptions, each with a plain clinical reading.11

    AssumptionPlain meaning in the exampleHow to probe it
    Exchangeability (no unmeasured confounding)After adjusting for the measured baseline factors, the schedule a patient got is unrelated to how long they would have lived under either scheduleThe DAG. Balance checks. Sensitivity analysis for what is missing.
    Positivity (overlap)Every kind of patient could plausibly have received either schedule. If every patient over 85 gets hypofractionation, the data say nothing about conventional fractionation at that age.Plot the estimated probability of hypofractionation in each group. Restrict the population where overlap fails.
    Consistency (a well-defined treatment)“Hypofractionation” means a specific family of schedules, not anything shorter than usualDefine the strategies by dose per fraction and number of fractions. Check that the effect does not depend on which schedule within the family was used.
    Two layers

    A causal analysis has two layers. Identification (this step) decides whether the data can answer the question. Estimation (step 7) decides how well you compute the answer. Better models, including neural networks, help the second layer. They cannot fix a failure of the first.

    6 · Treatment policies

    Compare the rules clinicians actually use

    “Everyone gets hypofractionation” versus “everyone gets conventional fractionation” is a static comparison. Clinicians rarely act that way. They follow rules, and a causal analysis can compare rules directly.

    Kind of strategyExampleQuestion it answers
    StaticAll eligible patients receive hypofractionationWhat if practice switched completely?
    DynamicHypofractionation if age is 70 or older or travel time exceeds one hour, conventional otherwiseIs this triage rule better than current practice, or than treating everyone the same way?
    Modified treatment policyShorten each patient's prescribed course by five fractions where the dose constraints still allow itWhat if we shifted practice a step, instead of all the way?
    Time-varyingReplan the course if weight loss during treatment exceeds 10%Decisions made during treatment, based on how the patient is doing

    A dynamic strategy is a rule that assigns treatment at each decision point from the patient's history up to that point.11 Longitudinal modified treatment policies (LMTP) go further: they define the effect of changing each patient's treatment as a function of the treatment the patient would otherwise have received and of their history, with treatment and confounders that change over time.12

    Trap: time-varying confounding

    Weight loss during treatment can be caused by the earlier fractions and can also trigger a change of plan. When earlier treatment affects a factor that then drives later treatment, traditional adjustment, such as entering weight loss as a time-varying covariate in a Cox model, can introduce bias even when every confounder is measured.11 The g-methods, which include the LMTP estimators, are built for this setting.12

    7 · Roadmap step 5: estimation

    Choose the estimator last, and put your machine learning inside it

    Once the estimand is identified, several estimators can compute it. They differ in what they model and what must be right for the answer to be right. Most need two prediction tasks, called nuisance models: a model of the outcome given baseline factors, and a model of the probability of receiving each schedule given baseline factors (the propensity score).13 This is where your random forests, XGBoost and neural networks go. They stop being the analysis and become components of it. For example, targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) is often paired with super learning, which combines machine-learning algorithms so that the estimate depends less on any one modeling assumption being right.3

    EstimatorWhat it modelsWhat must be rightWhat it gives you
    Cox model with a treatment term (for contrast)Hazard given treatment and covariatesProportional hazards, the right covariates, a correct formOne conditional hazard ratio
    Outcome regression (g-computation)Outcome given treatment and covariatesThe outcome modelAverage outcomes under each strategy, for example adjusted survival curves
    Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)Propensity scoreThe propensity model, and enough overlapWeighted outcomes or survival curves under each strategy
    Augmented IPTW (AIPW)BothEither model (doubly robust)Average effect. Flexible machine learning can be used for both models when combined with cross-fitting.14
    TMLEBoth, then a targeting stepEither model (doubly robust)Average effect. Often paired with super learning.3
    Double machine learning (DML)Both, with cross-fittingBoth models good enough. Their errors affect the estimate only at second order.Average effect, with random forests, lasso, boosting or neural networks for the nuisance models14
    LMTP estimators (TMLE and sequential doubly robust)Both, at each time pointEither model at each time pointEffects of modified and rule-based policies, including treatments that change over time12
    Causal forest and causal survival forest (CSF)Treatment effect as a function of covariates, with doubly robust correctionsOverlap, enough data for subgroupsWho benefits more (CATE) and the average effect. CSF handles censored outcomes, targeting RMST or survival probability.15,16

    “Doubly robust” means the estimate stays consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity model is correct, given the identification assumptions of step 5.11 Its larger practical benefit is that flexible machine learning can be used for both while the confidence interval still means what it says. Running two or three estimators that depend on different models is also one of the checks in step 8.

    Why this happens

    The covariates that mostly predict who receives hypofractionation add little to predicting survival, so the penalty shrinks them first. Shrinking them hands their share of the confounding to the schedule term. Prediction accuracy barely changes, so a model chosen for accuracy cannot see the problem. Double machine learning uses the lasso twice, once to predict survival and once to predict the schedule, each on held-out folds, and then relates the two sets of residuals. Errors in either prediction then affect the effect estimate only at second order. Here unpenalized least squares would also work, because the model is linear and the sample is large. The lesson is not to read an effect off a model tuned for prediction.

    8 · Roadmap steps 6 and 7: checks and interpretation

    Build confidence without an answer key

    A prediction is validated on held-out data. A causal estimate cannot be, so confidence comes from how much it withstands. A practical sequence:

    1. Overlap and balance. Plot the propensity scores by group, and check that the adjusted groups are similar on every baseline factor.
    2. Agreement across estimators. If TMLE, IPTW and a causal survival forest, which rely on different models, give similar answers, the result does not hinge on one modeling choice.
    3. A known truth is recovered. Simulate data with a planted effect that resembles your cohort, run the same pipeline, and confirm it returns the planted effect.
    4. A known falsehood gives nothing. Shuffle the treatment labels, or use an outcome the treatment cannot affect (a negative control). The estimate should be near zero.
    5. How wrong could it be? Ask how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to explain the result away. The E-value expresses this on the risk ratio scale.18 For regression-based estimates, another approach expresses it as the share of variation in treatment and outcome a hidden factor would need, compared against measured covariates such as age or stage.19

    None of these proves the answer is right. Together they show which conclusions survive and which depend on a single assumption. The CAST methods page shows step 5 for survival data: a simulated unmeasured confounder moves every method at once while the visible diagnostics still look healthy.

    Habits worth changing

    • Putting every available variable into the model. Adjust for baseline common causes from the DAG, and leave out what the treatment affects.
    • Reading the treatment effect from feature importance or SHAP. These describe how the model uses its inputs. They are not treatment effects.
    • Reporting a hazard ratio as “the effect”. Report survival or RMST differences at prespecified times.
    • Starting follow-up at the end of treatment. Start it where the target trial would randomize.
    • Choosing the model with the best C-index for an effect question. Choose the estimator for the estimand, then check it as in step 8.
