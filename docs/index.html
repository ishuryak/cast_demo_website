From confounding to trajectories

How a Causal Survival Forest (CSF) removes confounding, and how CAST extends it to a full treatment-effect trajectory, on simulated oncology data where the true effect is known.

Each patient cohort below is simulated, so we know the true average treatment effect (ATE) at every horizon (the solid black curve). The estimand is the survival-probability difference (the gap in the probability of surviving past each horizon, P(T>t | treated) − P(T>t | control)) at horizons 12–108 months, in a synthetic cohort with control median survival near 55 months. Two illustrative effect shapes are available: a plateau (treatment is protective throughout, so the survival gap rises, peaks, then slowly narrows as both arms approach low survival) and a reversal (treatment helps early but harms after about four years, so the survival curves cross and the survival-probability difference rises, peaks, then turns negative). The reversal is a stylized teaching curve, not an empirical one. Each cohort carries four confounders (age, stage, performance status, comorbidity count) that drive both prognosis and treatment assignment, a prognostic non-confounder (smoking, which worsens survival but does not affect who is treated), and two negative-control covariates (sex, ethnicity) that affect nothing. Move the confounding slider and toggle methods to see which estimators recover the truth.

Accuracy vs. truth (RMSE, survival-probability)

Lower is better. Watch Naive collapse as confounding rises while CSF stays accurate. CSF and CAST are scored in-sample (no train/test split), so their accuracy here is optimistic relative to real-world deployment.

Cox baseline

A single time-constant hazard ratio, with no trajectory. The proportional-hazards test flags when a single hazard ratio cannot hold across all horizons. The dotted purple Cox (marginal) curve on the plot standardizes the same model to the survival-probability scale; it is confounder-adjusted (so not biased by confounding) but, forced to be proportional, it tracks the truth on the plateau shape yet flatlines on the reversal, where it cannot bend to follow the crossing.

Confounder imbalance (SMD)

Standardized mean difference between arms. |SMD|>0.1 signals imbalance the naive comparison cannot fix. As confounding rises, the four confounders become imbalanced while smoking (a non-confounder) and the negative controls sex and ethnicity stay near zero.

Propensity overlap (positivity)

The estimated probability of treatment from the propensity forest. Good overlap (values away from 0 and 1) is the positivity condition that causal adjustment needs. As confounding rises, healthier patients are almost always treated and propensities spread toward 0 and 1, so the share beyond [0.05, 0.95] grows. This near-violation of positivity is what leaves CSF with residual bias at strong confounding. The [0.01, 0.99] clip rarely binds, so the residual reflects genuine overlap stress rather than the clip.

CAST trajectory

A smooth quadratic effect trajectory. The point fit uses generalized least squares (weight = Σ̂⁻¹) automatically when the cross-horizon covariance is well-conditioned, falling back to weighted least squares otherwise. On this survival-probability scale the horizons are far less correlated than on the cumulative-RMST scale, so the covariance is well-conditioned and generalized least squares engages.

The shaded 95% band is the covariance-aware band: it propagates the Ledoit–Wolf-shrunk cross-horizon influence-function covariance through the fit (a sandwich variance), so correlated horizons are handled correctly rather than treated as independent. The band is conditional on the fitted forests and does not propagate the forests' own estimation error. Peak time and effect are model-based summaries of the horizon-specific causal estimates, not separately identified causal quantities.

Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage

Shrinkage intensity λ and the cross-horizon covariance condition number, before → after shrinkage, that stabilizes the covariance feeding the band.

1 · The confounding problem

Healthier patients (younger, earlier-stage, better performance status, fewer comorbidities) are preferentially selected into treatment (confounding by indication). The Naive unadjusted survival-probability difference then measures treatment effect plus baseline prognosis, so it is biased upward and overstates benefit. At zero confounding it matches the truth; turn confounding up and it diverges.

2 · CSF adjusts for confounding

The Causal Survival Forest orthogonalizes against a propensity model and a censoring model (Neyman-orthogonal, like double machine learning), recovering the true ATE at each horizon under mild-to-moderate confounding and substantially reducing (though not fully removing) the bias under strong confounding, where treatment propensities are pushed toward 0 or 1 and overlap is stressed (a positivity limit; see the strong-confounding figures, where CSF sits above the truth but far below the naive curve). Two non-orthogonalized ML baselines are shown for contrast: the RSF S-learner (one survival forest with treatment as a feature), which can ignore treatment when splitting and so attenuates the effect toward zero even with no confounding, and the RSF T-learner (separate per-arm forests), which avoids that attenuation but, lacking a propensity model, extrapolates across arms and stays biased under confounding. Neither is orthogonalized; only CSF adjusts in a principled way.

3 · Baselines fall short

Cox gives one hazard ratio for all time and assumes proportional hazards; it cannot express a rising-then-plateauing or reversing effect. The S- and T-learner survival forests predict survival well but give no principled causal contrast, and fail in two different ways (effect attenuation versus cross-arm extrapolation).

4 · CAST extends CSF to a trajectory

CSF gives correlated point estimates across horizons. CAST forms the cross-horizon covariance from the CSF influence-function scores, stabilizes it with Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage, and fits a smooth quadratic in time. The point fit is generalized least squares β̂ = (XᵀΣ̂⁻¹X)⁻¹XᵀΣ̂⁻¹y, which engages automatically because the survival-probability horizons make Σ̂ well-conditioned (it falls back to weighted least squares when they do not), and the 95% band is a covariance-aware sandwich that propagates Σ̂. The result is a smooth effect trajectory whose clinical metrics (peak effect, time-to-peak) are model-based summaries of the per-horizon causal estimates rather than separately identified causal quantities.

Method provenance. The CSF fits use grf::causal_survival_forest (survival.probability target). The CAST layer (ledoit_wolf_shrinkage, the cross-horizon influence-function covariance, and the covariance-aware quadratic trajectory fit) is ported from the production glioma CAST pipeline.
