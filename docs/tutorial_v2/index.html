Option A or Option B?

Two active treatment options. One of them is chosen more often for the patients who were fitter to begin with. How much of the survival difference between them is the treatment, and how much is the head start?

An oncology framing of the CAST demo, on simulated cohorts where the true answer is known. Other versions of this demo: methods and diagnostics · clinical view · illustrated walkthrough.

Every week an oncologist chooses between two reasonable options, and every week the registry records which one was chosen and what happened next. Can that record tell us which option was better?

Why this page says Option A and Option B, and not "treatment" and "control". Outside a placebo trial, oncology rarely compares a treatment against nothing. It compares one defensible plan against another: a dose-escalated schedule against a standard one, concurrent chemoradiation against sequential, one systemic agent added to radiation rather than a different one, a shorter hypofractionated course against a longer conventional course. Both arms are active. Both are offered in good faith. The methods on this page work exactly the same way whichever pair you have in mind, so the page names them Option A (the more intensive choice) and Option B (the standard choice).

Why the record alone cannot answer it. The more intensive option goes disproportionately to the patients judged fit enough to tolerate it: younger, better performance status, fewer comorbidities. Those patients would have lived longer on either option. So the raw survival gap between the arms mixes what Option A did with the head start of the people who received it. That mixing is confounding by indication, and separating the two is the whole problem.

What goes in. Ordinary observational survival data. One row per patient: which option they received, how long they were followed, whether they died or were last seen alive, and the characteristics recorded before the decision was made.

What comes out. Not one summary number, but a curve over follow-up: out of 100 patients given Option A, how many more are alive at each point than would have been alive had the same 100 been given Option B. A positive value favors Option A at that time. A negative value favors Option B.

Why a single hazard ratio is not enough. Cox regression does adjust for recorded characteristics, but it compresses all of follow-up into one number, which is only faithful if the advantage holds steady. In a comparison of two active options it often does not. An intensified schedule can buy early local control and cost late cardiac or pulmonary mortality. The survival curves then cross, and averaging an early gain against a late loss returns roughly "no difference", which is wrong at every single time point. That is the case this page is built around, and it is the reason to estimate a trajectory rather than a number.

The two methods being graded. A causal survival forest (CSF) is a machine-learning estimator: it is a forest of decision trees adapted to estimate a treatment effect rather than to predict an outcome, it adjusts for the recorded characteristics, and it handles patients whose follow-up ended before they died. It gives an answer at one chosen time. CAST (Causal Analysis for Survival Trajectories) takes those separate single-time answers and fits one smooth curve through them, using the fact that the same patients contribute to every time point, so the result is a trajectory across follow-up instead of a row of disconnected dots.

Units used throughout. Everything on this page is in extra survivors per 100 patients, sometimes written percentage points. "+8 per 100 at 60 months" means that if 100 patients received Option A, eight more of them would be alive at five years than if those same 100 had received Option B.

How to read this honestly

  • The bands are optimistic, and this page shows by how much. Across all 24 scenarios the nominal 95% band contains the known truth – of the time. With no unrecorded factor it is –; with the strongest unrecorded factor it is –. A band drawn under an assumption that has been violated does not widen to warn you. It stays narrow and travels with the estimate.
  • Those percentages are not measured coverage, and are much less precise than they look. The 120 cells are 24 simulated cohorts, not 120 independent trials: the five follow-up times inside one panel come from one draw and move together, so whole panels hit or miss as a block. Scored over panels rather than cells, the overall figure carries an interval of roughly –. A real calibration study would redraw each scenario at many seeds, and no such study exists here.
  • Stage is in the simulation and is not in the balance card. The generator gives stage the largest influence of any characteristic on which option is chosen, larger than age. Its standardized difference is never computed, so it cannot be displayed. The balance card above is therefore incomplete in exactly the place an oncologist would look first.
  • The bands are pointwise, not whole-curve. Each one is about a single follow-up time. The chance that the entire trajectory lies inside its band is lower than the chance for any one point, and this page does not compute that.
  • Every panel is one simulated cohort at one random draw. Small differences between methods move from panel to panel. Nothing here is a benchmark and no method is being crowned.
  • The estimates are not split into training and test data. CSF and CAST are fitted and scored on the same patients, so their accuracy here flatters what they would do on new patients.
  • The causal survival forests are not tuned. The propensity model is tuned; the effect forests run at package defaults, because the tuning argument is ignored when the propensity is supplied from outside.
  • These patients are simulated, and no real treatment is being compared. Option A and Option B are abstract labels for the two arms of a simulation. The effect shapes were chosen to be legible, and the reversal is drawn sharper than most real crossings. Nothing on this page is evidence about any actual schedule, drug or dose.
For methodologists: what is actually being computed

Estimand

The target is the marginal difference in survival probability at each horizon under Option A versus Option B, averaged over the covariate distribution of the whole cohort: E[SA(t)] − E[SB(t)], where SA and SB are patient-level survival functions under each option. This is a marginal causal contrast over potential outcomes. It is not the observed between-arm comparison P(T>t | A) − P(T>t | B), which is the confounded quantity this page is about. Nothing in the estimand requires either arm to be untreated, which is why relabelling control as Option B changes no computation.

Estimators

Per-horizon effects come from grf::causal_survival_forest with target = "survival.probability", supplied an out-of-bag propensity from a grf::regression_forest. CAST fits an inverse-variance weighted quadratic through the per-horizon estimates. The cross-horizon covariance is built from the patient-aligned doubly robust influence scores and stabilized with Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage; the band is the resulting sandwich interval. It is pointwise at each horizon and conditional on the fitted forests: it does not propagate the forests' own estimation error, and it is not a simultaneous band over the trajectory. The two machine-learning baselines are random survival forests fitted as an S-learner (the option is one feature among many) and as a T-learner (a separate forest per arm). Neither is orthogonalized.

Tuning

The propensity regression forest is tuned with grf's built-in cross-validation. The causal survival forests are not tuned: grf applies tune.parameters only when it has to estimate the propensity itself, and here the propensity is supplied, so the forests run at grf defaults.

Coverage and its precision

Coverage is computed in the browser from the same scenarios.json that draws the figure, by asking at every horizon of every scenario whether the interval contains the exported truth. The interval reported beside it is a cluster interval over the 24 panels, not a binomial interval over the 120 cells, because the five horizons within a panel are not independent draws.

Baseline anchor

The Option B survival shown on this page is the observed Kaplan–Meier survival in the Option B arm. It is not covariate standardized, so under confounding it is itself a biased picture of what Option B does. It is shown as a scale anchor for the difference, and the number needed to treat quoted beside it is derived from the adjusted difference alone, which requires no baseline.

Scope

24 scenarios: 2 effect shapes × 4 levels of recorded imbalance × 3 levels of unrecorded imbalance, each at 5 horizons from 12 to 108 months, n = 2000 per cohort. No multiplicity adjustment is applied across the 120 intervals or the scenario grid.
