Did the treatment help, or were those patients healthier to begin with?

An interactive demo on simulated cancer cohorts. Because the patients are simulated, the true answer is known, so every method on this page can be graded against the truth instead of against the other methods.

Written for a clinical audience. The methods-facing version of this demo, with the estimator internals, is here.

A hospital looks back through its records and asks the question every clinician wants answered: did the patients who received this treatment live longer because of it?

The records alone cannot say. Doctors offer treatment to the patients they judge fit enough to benefit, so the treated group starts out healthier, and any survival gap between the groups mixes the effect of the treatment with the head start of the people who received it. That mixing is called confounding, and pulling the two apart is the problem this page is about.

What goes in. Ordinary observational survival data: a registry extract, a pull from electronic health records, a single-institution series. One row per patient, carrying the treatment given, how long the patient was followed, whether they died or were last seen alive, and the characteristics recorded at the start.

What comes out. Not one summary number, but a curve over time: out of 100 treated patients, how many more are alive at each point in follow-up than would have been alive without the treatment. That is the quantity plotted below, and it is what every method here is trying to estimate. Formally it is the difference between two whole-population survival probabilities, one in which everybody was treated and one in which nobody was, at each time point.

Why the usual tools are not enough. Comparing the groups directly measures the treatment plus the head start, so it answers the wrong question. Cox regression does adjust for the recorded characteristics, but it compresses all of follow-up into a single hazard ratio, which is only right if the benefit holds steady over time. Many benefits do not: a benefit can take months to appear, or peak and fade, or turn into a harm late. Averaging an early benefit against a late harm returns roughly "no effect", which is wrong at every single point in time. Machine-learning survival models predict well, but a good prediction is not a causal answer.

What the two methods on trial are. A causal survival forest (CSF) is a machine-learning method that estimates the treatment effect at one chosen time point while adjusting for the recorded characteristics and handling patients whose follow-up ended early. CAST (Causal Analysis for Survival Trajectories) takes those separate single-time-point estimates and fits one smooth curve through them, so the result is a trajectory over follow-up rather than a row of disconnected dots.

The solid black line is the truth, known because these patients are simulated. Everything else is an estimate trying to find it.

Can you trust the uncertainty band?

A 95% band should contain the truth about 95% of the time. Here the truth is known, so that claim can be checked directly instead of assumed. Each cell is one follow-up time in the scenario you have selected.

How far is each method from the truth?

Average distance from the known truth across follow-up, in extra survivors per 100 treated. Shorter is better. The number in bold is how many times worse than the best method on this panel.

Differences smaller than about 1 patient per 100 are noise from this being a single simulated cohort, so read the ordering rather than the decimals.

Is this cohort confounded?

How far apart the two groups start out, on each recorded characteristic. Anything beyond 0.10 is an imbalance the raw comparison cannot fix.

Were both treatments genuinely available?

Adjustment only works where patients of a given type could plausibly have received either treatment. If the healthiest patients are always treated, there is nobody comparable to learn from.

–

What no adjustment can reach

This cohort carries a hidden fitness factor that drives both who gets treated and who survives, and it is withheld from every model, just as an unrecorded factor would be in a real study.

How to read this honestly

  • The uncertainty bands are optimistic, and the figure above shows by how much. Across all 24 scenarios in this demo the nominal 95% band contains the known truth – of the time. With no hidden confounding it is –; with strong hidden confounding it is –. A band drawn under an assumption that has been violated does not widen to warn you. It stays narrow and moves off the truth with the estimate.
  • The bands are pointwise, not whole-curve. Each one is about a single follow-up time. The chance that the entire trajectory lies inside its band is lower than the chance for any one time point, and this page does not compute that.
  • Every panel is one simulated cohort at one random draw. Small differences between methods move from panel to panel. Nothing here is a performance benchmark, and no method is being crowned.
  • The estimates are not split into training and test data. CSF and CAST are fitted and scored on the same patients, so their accuracy here is better than it would be on genuinely new patients.
  • The peak timing carries no uncertainty interval. It is read off the fitted curve, and where the true effect reverses it lands systematically late. Treat it as a description of the curve that was fitted, not as an estimate of when the benefit truly peaks.
  • These patients are simulated, not real. The effect shapes were chosen to be legible, and the reversal shape is drawn sharper than most real crossings so each method's response to it is visible. Nothing on this page is evidence about any actual treatment.
For methodologists: what is actually being computed

Estimand

The target is the marginal difference in survival probability at each horizon under treatment versus control, averaged over the covariate distribution of the whole cohort: E[S₁(t)] − E[S₀(t)], where S₁ and S₀ are the patient-level survival functions under treatment and control. This is a marginal causal contrast over potential outcomes, not the observed between-group comparison P(T>t | treated) − P(T>t | control), which is the confounded quantity the page is about.

Estimators

Per-horizon effects come from grf::causal_survival_forest with target = "survival.probability", supplied an out-of-bag propensity from a grf::regression_forest. CAST fits an inverse-variance weighted quadratic through the per-horizon estimates. The cross-horizon covariance is built from the patient-aligned doubly robust influence scores and stabilized with Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage; the band is the resulting sandwich interval. It is pointwise at each horizon and conditional on the fitted forests: it does not propagate the forests' own estimation error, and it is not a simultaneous band over the trajectory.

Tuning

The propensity regression forest is tuned with grf's built-in cross-validation. The causal survival forests are not tuned: grf applies tune.parameters only when it has to estimate the propensity itself, and here the propensity is supplied, so the forests run at grf defaults. Any statement elsewhere that all forests are tuned does not hold for the causal survival forests.

Coverage

The coverage figures on this page are computed in the browser from the same scenarios.json that draws the figure, by asking at every horizon of every scenario whether the interval contains the exported truth. They are not transcribed, so they cannot drift from the data.

Scope

24 scenarios: 2 effect shapes × 4 levels of measured confounding × 3 levels of unmeasured confounding, each at 5 horizons from 12 to 108 months, n = 2000 per cohort. No multiplicity adjustment is applied across the 120 intervals, the 24 proportional-hazards tests, or the scenario grid.
