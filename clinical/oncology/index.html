Patients receiving A live longer. It looks like a better treatment. But the apparent advantage hides two different problems.

A looks better.

In this simulated cohort, patients receive one of two active treatment options, A or B. At every displayed follow-up time, survival is higher among those who received A.

The line shows that observed gap: the difference in survivors per 100 patients. It is tempting to read it as the benefit of treatment.

But who received A?

Fitter patients were more likely to receive A. They also tended to live longer either way. Part of the apparent advantage was there before treatment began.

Because these patients are simulated, we can ask what would happen if the same population received each option. The answer contains a second surprise: A improves early survival, but the survival difference later reverses.

Time changes the answer.

The adjusted Cox model below assumes a constant treatment hazard ratio. It adjusts for recorded patient characteristics, yet its single treatment coefficient cannot represent this reversal. Adjustment and flexibility solve different problems.

What proportional hazards assumes

A proportional-hazards model with one constant treatment coefficient assumes a constant treatment hazard ratio. It can still produce a survival-probability difference that changes with time. What this fitted specification cannot reproduce is the reversal shown here: the same population’s survival under A starts above B and ends below it. Cox models with time-varying treatment effects can relax that restriction.

The proportional-hazards assumption and its diagnostics ↗

The pipeline’s treatment-term PH test flags this mismatch in the fixed story cohort (p < 0.0001, from the rounded export). A diagnostic can alert an analyst to the restriction; a more flexible specification can then be considered.

Let the effect change with time.

A causal survival forest estimates the difference at each selected horizon without requiring a constant treatment hazard ratio. It adjusts for recorded characteristics and accounts for censoring. Here, its estimates follow the rise and reversal more closely.

CAST connects those snapshots with a quadratic curve and carries their shared uncertainty into the result. We can read a trajectory rather than five isolated estimates.

The curve also shows the cost of simplification. It smooths past the early peak. At 84 months, its estimate and interval remain positive even though the known difference is negative. CAST makes a time pattern easier to interpret; it does not guarantee a better fit.

In a real study, the diamonds disappear.

We would need well-defined treatment options, adequate treatment and follow-up support, and defensible assumptions about confounding and censoring. Neither method can adjust for a common cause that was never recorded.

This is one simulated cohort, not a ranking of methods for every study. A and B are abstract options, and the changing effect of a choice made at baseline is not a rule for when to stop or switch treatment.

Which pointwise intervals contain the truth in this one simulated cohort? Each cell is one follow-up horizon. These five related checks do not measure 95% repeated-sampling coverage.

How far is each method from the truth?

Root-mean-square distance from the known truth across follow-up, in extra survivors per 100. Shorter is better.

How different were the two arms to begin with?

Standardized mean difference between the Option A arm and the Option B arm on each characteristic the models can see. A magnitude of 0.10 is a common descriptive flag, not a test of exchangeability.

Were both options genuinely on the table?

Adjustment only works where a patient of a given type could plausibly have received either option. If the fittest patients always get Option A, there is nobody comparable to learn from.

What no adjustment can reach

At the higher settings this cohort carries an unrecorded fitness factor that drives both which option was chosen and how long the patient survived. It is withheld from every model, exactly as an unrecorded factor would be in a real study.

What this simulation can and cannot show
  • Containment in this frozen grid. CAST intervals contain the truth in – of the displayed cells: – with no hidden confounding and – at the strongest hidden setting. These descriptive counts do not estimate coverage for any one scenario. Intervals need not account for bias from violated assumptions.
  • Calibration needs repeated simulations. There is one draw per scenario and five correlated horizons per draw. Repeated seeds at each fixed scenario would be needed to assess interval coverage and method performance. The grid is not a random sample of clinical studies.
  • Stage is in the simulation and is not in the balance card. Stage influences treatment assignment and is included in the fitted models. Its standardized difference is never computed, so it cannot be displayed. The balance card therefore does not summarize all recorded baseline characteristics.
  • The bands are pointwise, not whole-curve. Each one is about a single follow-up time. The chance that the entire trajectory lies inside its band can be lower than the chance for any one point, and this page does not compute that.
  • Every panel is one simulated cohort at one random draw. Small differences between methods move from panel to panel. Nothing here is a benchmark and no method is being crowned.
  • This is not an external validation. The average-effect calculation uses grf’s doubly robust scores and out-of-bag machinery. Evaluating this cohort-average target does not itself require a separate patient test set. Repeated simulation and separate validation of any learned clinical prediction or treatment rule remain different tasks.
  • The causal survival forests are not tuned. The propensity model is tuned; the effect forests use specified tree counts and seeds, with other settings at package defaults. The tuning argument applies only to internal propensity estimation, which is bypassed when a propensity is supplied.
  • These patients are simulated, and no real treatment is being compared. Option A and Option B are abstract labels for the two arms of a simulation. The effect shapes were chosen for teaching and are not calibrated to a clinical population. Nothing on this page is evidence about any actual schedule, drug or dose.
For methodologists: what is actually being computed

Estimand

The target is the marginal difference in survival probability at each horizon under Option A versus Option B, averaged over the covariate distribution of the whole cohort: E[SA(t)] − E[SB(t)], where SA and SB are patient-level survival functions under each option. This is a marginal causal contrast over potential outcomes. It is not the observed between-arm comparison P(T>t | A) − P(T>t | B), which is the confounded quantity this page is about. Nothing in the estimand requires either arm to be untreated, which is why relabelling control as Option B changes no computation.

Estimators

Per-horizon effects come from grf::causal_survival_forest with target = "survival.probability", supplied an out-of-bag propensity from a grf::regression_forest. CAST fits a quadratic through the per-horizon estimates. All 24 exported scenarios use generalized least squares (GLS); the source has an inverse-variance weighted fallback when its GLS acceptance checks fail. The cross-horizon covariance is built from the patient-aligned doubly robust influence scores and stabilized with Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage; the band is the resulting sandwich interval. The sandwich covariance propagates the estimated cross-horizon score covariance through the quadratic fit. These are nominal pointwise intervals, not simultaneous bands. Their calibration also depends on identification, nuisance estimation, covariance estimation and the adequacy of the quadratic approximation; a fixed-forest-bootstrap description would refer to an older implementation. The two machine-learning baselines are random survival forests fitted as an S-learner (the option is one feature among many) and as a T-learner (a separate forest per arm). Neither is orthogonalized.

Tuning

The propensity regression forest is tuned with grf's built-in cross-validation. The causal survival forests are not tuned: grf applies tune.parameters only when it has to estimate the propensity itself. Here the propensity is supplied; the effect forests use specified tree counts and seeds, with other settings at defaults.

Containment and coverage

The browser checks whether each interval contains the exported truth. Pooled hit counts describe this particular grid, not calibrated coverage. No confidence interval for pooled coverage is reported, because the fixed scenarios are not exchangeable replicate samples from a stated target distribution.

Observed Option B survival

The Kaplan–Meier estimate describes survival among those who actually received B, under its censoring assumptions. It is not survival under B standardized to the whole target cohort. We do not combine it with the adjusted survival difference to report a causal relative change or E-value.

Scope

24 scenarios: 2 effect shapes × 4 levels of recorded imbalance × 3 levels of unrecorded imbalance, each at 5 horizons from 12 to 108 months, n = 2000 per cohort. No multiplicity adjustment is applied across the 120 intervals or the scenario grid.

Reading paths

How would you describe yourself?

Suggested stops for different questions.

Start with the choice you actually make.

Read the effect in patients per 100, then find out what it would take to overturn it.

  1. See the difference between the two options over follow-up
  2. Read it as extra survivors and as a number needed to treat
  3. Ask what an unrecorded factor would do to all of it

Start with the estimate and its uncertainty.

Look at what the interval claims, then at how often that claim held.

  1. Compare each estimator against the known truth
  2. Grade the 95% interval instead of assuming it
  3. Rank the estimators by distance from truth

Start with the estimand.

Fix what is being estimated, then look at the estimators and their failure modes.

  1. Read the estimand and the estimators
  2. Compare all six estimators on the same panel
  3. Check interval calibration across the whole grid

Start where the arms differ.

Begin with baseline imbalance, the familiar diagnostic, then follow it to what adjustment cannot reach.

  1. Look at baseline imbalance between the arms
  2. Ask whether both options were genuinely available
  3. Meet the factor no adjustment can use

Prepare a session.

Open with the question, demonstrate the failure live, then close on what the demo does not establish.

  1. Drive the confounding controls in front of a group
  2. See what hidden confounding changes
  3. Close on what this cannot show
